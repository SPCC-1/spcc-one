01 · EL BRONX · 1940s

The market street

Later on I was to learn that our move was part of a larger exodus of Puerto Ricans from the over-crowded Barrio and that this move, if not economically, at least psychologically, represented a kind of upward mobility. We had moved into an area that had few Puerto Ricans. It was poor but most of the families were still Jewish, Irish, Greek and Italian. As the area became more Puerto Rican, tropical fruits and vegetables began to dominate the scene.

Excerpted from “El Bronx,” manuscript, p. 15–17. Lightly shortened for this page.

02 · HIGH SCHOOL LIFE · 1948

A teacher opens a door

When I entered Morris High School in September of 1948, I was on the brink of a serious development in my life. I believe that one of the strong factors pulling me toward academic achievement was again my teachers. Mr. Maloff became an idol for me—one of those people who make a deep impression on you and whom you want to emulate. He encouraged untrammeled discussion and demonstrated a faith in us. He was hard with people who just voiced opinions without prior analysis and research.

Excerpted from “High School Life,” manuscript, p. 45. Lightly shortened for this page.

Guiding question

Who helps a young person imagine a different future—and what does that support look like?

03 · COLLEGE DAYS · 1950s

Learning the way in

Going to college had not even been a remote thought to me until I was about half way through high school. The impetus to go to college came from the teachers who took an interest in me and who just assumed that I would go to college. A large part of the motivation and know-how about going to a city college came from a group of college kids who lived on my block. They actually oriented me to the mechanics of getting into college. The help that they gave me was invaluable.

Excerpted from “College Days,” manuscript, p. 60. Lightly shortened for this page.

04 · EL BRONX · THE BLOCK

Frankie on the block

Somehow or other an argument broke out between Frankie and me. I don’t quite remember the origin but I do remember the result. Since Frankie was bigger than I was, I picked up a stick to help me fight. When Frankie still came at me all of my brothers, sisters, and cousins joined the fray. Frankie not only became a close personal friend but he almost became a member of our family.

Excerpted from “El Bronx,” manuscript, p. 17. Lightly shortened for this page.

05 · EL BRONX · FAMILY

Eight children, many shades

The eight of us standing side by side looked quite different in physical appearance. Some of us were quite fair in complexion, others had more of an olive or tan look. The hair on some was smooth as silk, on others it was wildly curly or even kinky. The lovely thing is that we were all brothers and sisters and no one was better than anyone else on the basis of color or looks.

Excerpted from “El Bronx,” manuscript, p. 18. Lightly shortened for this page.

06 · COLLEGE DAYS · A CLOSED DOOR

When a future was refused

A law firm in the financial district had indicated that they were considering employing a young college student as a clerk who was interested in the legal profession. I was recommended enthusiastically by the people at Morris and I was sent for an interview. The interview went well until near the end when the lawyer happened to ask my religion. When he found out that I wasn’t Jewish, he very neatly explained that his firm had to hire Jewish students because the other firms also practiced religious and ethnic favoritism.

Excerpted from “College Days,” manuscript, p. 61. Lightly shortened for this page.

Guiding question

What happens when a young person's effort meets a system designed to keep a door closed?

07 · HIGH SCHOOL LIFE · STREET CULTURE

The gang, the street, and school

When I entered Morris High School in September of 1948, I was on the brink of a serious development in my life. I was not yet a devoted student. I still met with my gang buddies and there was still the risk of getting into trouble with the police. School did not mean much to me in the earlier grades because the street had more relevance for me.

I am sure that I could not have survived if I had not had a previous connection with the gang members and continued to retain their respect. Even with this relationship, I was still taunted for being bookish and a “good” boy. There were times when I would have to remove my eyeglasses and revert to coarse language to prove that I was hip and could take care of myself. Having younger brothers who were now active in the gangs made my position more palatable. Another saving factor was that I continued to play basketball, stick ball, and other sports with the guys. Since I had some skill in sports, I was in some demand as a ball player and I was never considered a flunky, sissy, or faggot.

This potential conflict between school and society is especially keen in low-income areas. From my experience, concerns about masculine self-image applied across the board to the Irish, Italian, Jewish, and Negro low-income kids that I knew. Unless parents, policemen, and school authorities are conscious of their effects, all the lecturing and punishing in the world is no match for the need to survive as a social being in a neighborhood.

Excerpted from “High School Life,” manuscript, p. 45, 49–50. Lightly shortened for this page.

Guiding question

What did Alfredo have to learn in order to move between the street, school, and the future he wanted?

08 · EL BRONX · FAMILY LIFE

A house full of people

Our house was always full of people—if not the boys’ friends, then the girls’ friends. It seemed to me that a party atmosphere hung in the air at all times. My mother was gay and lively and she would eagerly play her records after school or in the evening. Thus we had the makings of a dance almost instantly.

Despite the fact that we were ten in all in the apartment, we almost always had a boarder. Uncle Fico, Uncle Filin, and others stayed with us from time to time. We used to love to have them stay, particularly Tio Titin, because he would bring us candy and play games with us. When a boarder or visitor was staying for a short time, he slept on the couch in the living room. For a longer stay, some of us would double up in order to provide a bed.

The extended family had gatherings rather frequently, either in Uncle Chato’s house or in our apartment. On Friday or Saturday nights the men would gather to play dominoes. All that was needed was a little music, some soda, and some food and a swinging party would develop.

Excerpted from “El Bronx,” manuscript, p. 18–20. Lightly shortened for this page.

Guiding question

How can a crowded apartment become an institution of care, culture, and belonging?

09 · EL BRONX · FOOD & MEMORY

Pasteles, guests, and the precious piece of meat

My mother always cooked huge amounts in a large pot so that no matter who came to the house there was always nice rice and beans for them. Meat, of course, was more of a precious commodity. Whenever unexpected guests came in, my mother would offer them a meal and then proceed to scour up whatever meat she could.

The family gatherings that I loved most were around the Christmas and New Year’s Eve holidays. My mother and the other women would make special meat pies, which we called pasteles, and other goodies like coquito, a coconut drink, majarete, a rice meal custard, and pudin de pan, a delicious bread pudding. Pasteles required the grating of tropical vegetables such as plantains, yautias, and green bananas to make the masa or dough.

Although my mother generally made more than a hundred, they never lasted too long. The pasteles were coupled with lechón asado, roast pork, and arroz con gandules. These were typical meals repeated at other special occasions, like a homecoming for someone returning from Puerto Rico or a farewell.

Excerpted from “El Bronx,” manuscript, p. 20–21. Lightly shortened for this page.

Guiding question

What can food memories reveal about scarcity, generosity, migration, and cultural survival?
