Public education and the American experiment

Where should power live?

America has always been an argument between local voice and common purpose, freedom from distant authority and the capacity to act together. Public education is where generations have practiced that argument—and where Civic Labs can help us hold both sides.

The argument at the center

Public education is where America rehearses democracy.

The American experiment has never been a finished design. It is renegotiated through conflict, reform, and adaptation. Polarization is not new to that experiment, and disagreement is not itself a democratic failure. The failure is losing the institutions, habits, and relationships that let people encounter difference, deliberate about common problems, and make consequential decisions together.

The enduring tension

Local voice + common purpose

The danger is both concentrated power and fragmented power.

One American tradition asks how power can be kept close enough to the people to remain accountable. Another asks how communities can coordinate at the scale collective action requires. Concentrated authority can flatten local knowledge and become unresponsive. Fragmented authority can leave institutions unable to protect rights, distribute resources fairly, or act on problems no neighborhood can solve alone.

The question has never simply been whether to centralize or decentralize. It is what must be held in common, what should be decided at the closest competent level, and what civic infrastructure can connect the two without allowing either to dominate.

Public schools have carried this tension for more than a century. They are local institutions with a universal public charge: places where plural communities meet, where young people encounter the unfinished arguments of the country, and where civic engagement and dialogue must be learned through practice.

1950s–1960s

Equal protection

Brown v. Board of Education made equality a public obligation.

In 1954, the Supreme Court declared that legally segregated public schools violated the Equal Protection Clause. The decision established a boundary that local custom and local majorities could not cross: a child’s right to equal public education could not depend on the willingness of a district or community to provide it.

Brown therefore required power beyond the locality. Federal courts, state governments, and public systems had to possess enough authority to enforce rights, dismantle legalized exclusion, and hold institutions to a standard shared across communities. Equality could not be secured through local discretion alone.

But desegregation also exposed the limits of authority exercised from above. Formal access did not guarantee belonging, responsive institutions, fair resources, culturally relevant learning, or a meaningful voice for families whose schools had long been governed without them. The constitutional settlement answered whether separation could be lawful. It did not answer who should shape the integrated public institution that followed.

What this era adds to the braid. Some guarantees must be held in common and enforced beyond the local level. But equal access without voice leaves the design and daily experience of public education unresolved.

1960s–1970s

Decentralization

New York City moved the struggle from access to authority.

In the decade after Brown, Black and Puerto Rican communities confronted a school system that was formally bound by equality yet remained deeply segregated, bureaucratic, and distant from the people it served. The next question was not only whether children could enter public schools, but who would have the authority to define what happened inside them.

New York City’s community-control battles made that question institutional. Parents and organizers demanded power over leadership, staffing, curriculum, priorities, resources, and accountability. The 1969 decentralization law created thirty-two community school districts—an attempt to move meaningful authority closer to neighborhoods while retaining a central system responsible for the city as a whole.

Alfredo Mathew Jr. worked inside that constitutional and institutional tension. A Bronx teacher and principal, he became New York City’s first Puerto Rican community superintendent. He later served as Executive Director of Community School Affairs, carrying responsibility across the city’s decentralized system. His work required him to move between two legitimate demands: the authority of communities to shape their schools and the obligation of the larger public system to protect rights, distribute resources fairly, and maintain common responsibility.

He argued that education could not be separated from poverty, hunger, public safety, culture, and neighborhood wellbeing. But he also saw that local control alone could reproduce inequity, patronage, or isolation if it lacked clear public standards and accountability. The task was not to choose community over system. It was to make the system answerable to communities while preserving the capacity to guarantee justice across them.

What this era adds to the braid. Equality requires a shared public floor; democracy requires meaningful local authority. Neither a distant center nor fragmented local control can carry the whole obligation. The next era would need institutions capable of holding both at once.

1970s–1980s

Community Schools

Community Schools made the neighborhood part of the school—and became the model.

Drawing on much older traditions of settlement houses, neighborhood schools, and full-service education, the foundations of the modern Community School movement took shape through the 1970s and 1980s, then expanded through the decades that followed. It rejected the idea that schools could educate children while remaining separate from family wellbeing, health, housing, employment, culture, and the organizations already holding community trust.

The innovation was not simply to place more programs inside a school. It was to make partnership part of the operating model: a coordinator connecting educators, families, community organizations, public agencies, and resources around the needs and aspirations of a particular place. The school remained public and accountable to shared commitments, while the work became more locally informed and locally connected.

A parallel movement in higher education was learning how to make colleges more accountable to place. Centers for community service, public service, and civic engagement organized service-learning, community-based research, national service, and reciprocal partnerships as part of an institution’s educational mission. At their best, these centers did more than send volunteers outward: they built durable relationships through which community priorities could shape teaching, scholarship, and the use of university resources.

Amy Chan’s work reflects this strand of the field. At the University of Chicago’s University Community Service Center, she led a civic-engagement portfolio serving thousands of students through thirteen programs and more than 250 community partnerships. The deeper lesson was institutional: community engagement requires staff, trusted partners, shared expectations, and continuity. Goodwill becomes public capacity only when an institution builds a structure able to hold the relationship.

Across these formative decades, place-based experiments established a durable operating form: public schools working with families and community partners around the conditions young people needed to learn and thrive. What had often depended on an exceptional principal or temporary program began to become an institutional model.

What this era adds to the braid. The school can operate as a neighborhood hub, but partnership must become part of its durable public design—not an assortment of services attached to the edge of instruction. Later generations would inherit that structure and bring it toward public scale.

1990s–2000s

School design

The small-schools movement brought public education closer to the learner.

By the 1980s, another critique of centralized schooling had taken shape. Ted Sizer’s study of the American high school exposed institutions trying to cover too much, asking too little intellectually, and allowing both students and teachers to become anonymous. In 1984, he founded the Coalition of Essential Schools at Brown University—a national network organized around a different proposition: schools should be small enough to know their students, intellectually focused enough to help them use their minds well, and flexible enough for teachers to shape learning around the young people before them.

The Coalition’s principles made the classroom more democratic. “Less is more” favored depth over superficial coverage. Students became workers rather than passive recipients; teachers became coaches rather than lecturers. Mastery was demonstrated through exhibitions and consequential performances, not reduced to seat time or standardized recall. Personalization, trust, intellectual challenge, and equity were treated as parts of one design.

Deborah Meier made this philosophy concrete in New York City. Through Central Park East and the schools and educators influenced by it, she showed that rigorous learning could be organized around democratic relationships and durable Habits of Mind: examining evidence, considering viewpoint, making connections, imagining alternatives, and asking why something matters. These were not merely academic strategies. They were habits for participating in a pluralistic democracy.

The Bronx small-schools movement carried that lineage into communities historically underserved by large, impersonal institutions. Schools such as Fannie Lou Hamer Freedom High School demonstrated how sustained inquiry, performance-based assessment, advisory relationships, internships, and public exhibitions could make students known and make their thinking visible. Mott Haven Village Prep emerged within that wider ecology of educators, reform networks, community organizations, and new schools.

High Tech High in San Diego carried these ideas into a visible school-design prototype. Interdisciplinary projects, teaching teams, internships, public exhibitions, and work made for audiences beyond the teacher showed what Deeper Learning could look like as an entire school rather than an exceptional classroom. The Hewlett Foundation helped name and support Deeper Learning as a national field, connecting High Tech High with other school networks around the capacity to think critically, collaborate, communicate, learn how to learn, and apply knowledge in unfamiliar situations.

The Buck Institute for Education—now PBLWorks—helped translate that aspiration into a disciplined teaching practice. Its frameworks, professional learning, and standards for high-quality Project Based Learning gave educators a common language for designing sustained inquiry, authentic problems, critique, revision, student voice, and public products. XQ Institute then widened the frame again: inviting communities to redesign high school itself around meaningful learning, caring relationships, youth voice, flexible uses of time and space, and partnerships beyond the school walls.

Alfred Solis worked across all three layers: as a teacher, facilitator, and Digital Commons lead at High Tech High; as Director of Innovation and Special Projects at Buck Institute; and as Head of Educator Excellence at XQ. His journey makes the field’s development visible. A powerful local prototype becomes a transferable practice; the practice becomes professional infrastructure; the infrastructure becomes a national invitation to redesign institutions. The persistent challenge is preserving the depth and relationships of the original practice as it moves toward scale.

Alfredo Mathew III entered this movement as a Bronx teacher and school co-founder. In a 2013 letter published by The New York Times, “Nine High Schools, One Roof,” he reflected on both the promise and the unresolved tensions of replacing large comprehensive schools with smaller schools sharing a campus. Smallness could create relationship and intellectual community, but it could not by itself guarantee equitable resources, sustain collaboration across schools, or connect strong student work to the public decisions shaping community life.

What this era adds to the braid. Young people produce rigorous work when they are known, trusted, challenged, and asked to demonstrate what they understand. The next era would need to carry those relationships and habits beyond the school—connecting learning to colleges, workplaces, community institutions, and consequential public work.

2000s–2010s

Pathways

College, career, and workforce movements connected learning to life beyond school.

For decades, educators, employers, colleges, workforce boards, unions, intermediaries, and community organizations have worked to make the passage from school into adulthood more coherent. College-and-career readiness widened the definition of preparation. Career and technical education joined academic knowledge to skilled practice. Workforce development organized training around regional labor markets. Work-based learning added internships, apprenticeships, mentorship, client projects, and other experiences through which young people could learn with adults doing consequential work.

Citizen Schools showed how this bridge could be built outside the conventional school day and at national scale. Its apprenticeship model recruited professionals and community members as “Citizen Teachers,” turning workplaces, technical knowledge, and adult networks into learning environments for middle-school students. Young people did not simply hear about possible futures; they worked with adults to make, investigate, perform, and present something real. The model demonstrated that communities already contain enormous educational capacity if an intermediary can organize it around sustained relationships and a shared learning experience.

Amy Chan helped build this strand from more than one position: leading Citizen Schools summer and after-school programs, mobilizing community volunteers as apprenticeship teachers, and later supporting a multi-school STEM pilot that connected teachers with engineers, scientists, and software developers. Her experience carries a central lesson into Civic Labs: the people and expertise exist, but participation becomes a pathway only when someone creates the structure, prepares both sides, and sustains the relationship.

Deeper Learning and project-based learning strengthened this bridge from the classroom side. They emphasized interdisciplinary inquiry, collaboration, communication, problem-solving, reflection, and public demonstrations of learning—the habits and capabilities people need to navigate institutions, contribute to teams, and continue learning across a lifetime.

Entrepreneurship education added another dimension: young people as initiators and creators, not only future employees. NFTE was one expression of this wider movement. It extended learning into businesses and regional opportunity networks, enabling young people to develop ideas, present to professionals, build plans, and practice agency before audiences with knowledge and resources beyond school.

Together, these traditions built pathways into college, careers, professions, and enterprise. They also revealed an important limit. A pathway can help an individual enter an existing economy without changing who owns assets, whose knowledge counts, or whether value remains in the community that helped produce it.

What this era adds to the braid. A pathway should do more than help an individual enter an existing economy. The next era must connect learning and contribution to ownership, community value, and shared prosperity.

2020s

Ownership

Entrepreneurship became a mass pathway. Ownership remained unequally distributed.

Across the country, the boundaries between worker, entrepreneur, contractor, and business owner have blurred. Independent work and digital platforms lowered some barriers to earning and enterprise. The gig economy expanded. More people began selling their labor, knowledge, products, and services directly—and the United States became a nation of more than 36 million small businesses, the great majority operating without employees.

This rise in entrepreneurship reflects initiative and a desire for agency. It also transfers risk. A person can own a job without owning an asset, control a schedule without controlling the platform, or start a business without access to patient capital, procurement, property, networks, or the institutional support required to grow. Entry into entrepreneurship is not the same as durable ownership.

The ownership-economy and community-economic-development traditions ask the larger questions: Who owns the productive assets? Who governs investment? Where does the value created by local labor and knowledge accumulate? Can enterprise build community wealth, or does it simply create another precarious pathway through an economy whose rules and infrastructure remain beyond community control?

ESO Ventures emerged as one response. Built through East Oakland relationships, it began from a simple recognition: talent and ambition were abundant; aligned capital and infrastructure were not. Its founders and partners connected culturally responsive entrepreneurial development to grants, investment, markets, and long-term support for Black and Brown business owners.

Capital in the Community carried that response into public policy. California’s 2021 budget committed $8 million to the City of Oakland for ESO Ventures under the California Capital in the Community Act, followed by $10 million for the California Entrepreneurship Capital in the Community Initiative. The proposition was larger than a program: public capital could be organized through trusted local infrastructure so communities could develop entrepreneurs, circulate resources, and build assets that remain rooted in place.

What this era adds to the braid. Communities do not lack ideas, initiative, or social capital. They need systems that recognize contribution, broaden ownership, and let locally created value remain, accumulate, and become an inheritance.

The public platform we inherited

The field advances by braiding what each generation has learned.

We do not need to invent civic infrastructure from nothing. We need to connect the public institutions, community relationships, learning practices, and pathways that generations have already built.

Progressive education contributed inquiry and learning through experience. Community-control movements insisted that public institutions answer to the people closest to the consequences. Community Schools made partnership part of the school’s operating design. Small schools joined rigor to relationship and public demonstrations of learning. College, career, workforce, and entrepreneurial movements connected learning to life beyond school. Community economic development added ownership and locally retained value.

Each movement solved part of the problem and revealed the next limit. Braided together, their accumulated social capital becomes public capacity: people who trust one another, institutions that know how to work together, transferable practices that cross sectors, and knowledge the next generation can inherit.

That inheritance now has national reach. New York City has 419 Community Schools across every district. California’s $4.1 billion initiative reaches nearly 2,500 schools, with another $1 billion in ongoing funding committed in 2026. Across the country, Community Schools, community colleges, public agencies, and neighborhood organizations already hold the places, relationships, people, and public investment from which a civic learning system can grow.

The lesson of this history is not “centralize” or “decentralize.” It is clearer than that: protect what everyone is owed, let communities shape the work closest to them, and make the results visible enough for the wider public to learn from.

To become a civic learning system, this infrastructure needs three connections.

1 · A shared public floor

Public systems protect the rights, resources, access, and standards every learner and community should be able to count on.

2 · Real community authority

Communities define the questions that matter; schools and partners shape the relationships, learning, and response around local knowledge.

3 · Visible, reusable evidence

Public work is transparently documented, tested, attributed, and preserved—so learners receive recognition and communities gain trustworthy assets others can find, use, and build upon.

Civic Labs connect these three conditions: a common commitment, locally grounded work, and public evidence that becomes shared memory. That is how the infrastructure we already have becomes a civic learning system.
