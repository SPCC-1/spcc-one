Civic Labs and Community Schools: The Gap and the Opportunity

Building on what the Bronx Economic Democracy Fellowship proved—and creating the civic infrastructure needed to make authentic community learning durable, visible, and valuable.

Work already done

The Bronx has already built the foundation.

The Bronx Economic Democracy Schools Fellowship brought together five schools, 13 students, five educator fellows, and 18 months of development and delivery. Led through the Bronx Cooperative Development Initiative with The Point CDC, Northwest Bronx Community and Clergy Coalition—now Our Bronx—and Bronx educators and young people, it showed how schools can become sites for learning and practicing economic democracy.

The Bronx is not simply the location of this work; it is one of the places where American school reform has repeatedly been tested and remade. Our Bronx carries more than 50 years of organizing born from the borough’s struggle against abandonment and disinvestment, while Bronx educators built smaller, more democratic schools around relationship, inquiry, public work, and social action. Fannie Lou Hamer Freedom High School, opened in 1994 and rooted in the Coalition of Essential Schools tradition, joined the legacy of the Mississippi Freedom Schools to the realities of the South Bronx. The Economic Democracy Fellowship extends that lineage: it asks not only how a school can become more humane and intellectually serious, but how young people can help shape the economy and civic life of the place they call home.

A longer reform lineage

Schools have been reaching toward authentic learning for generations.

The fellowship belongs to a century-long effort to connect learning with experience, democracy, and community life. Each movement moved the work forward. None fully built the durable bridge between schools and civic institutions.

Progressive education

The national model

Beginning in the early twentieth century, progressive educators argued that students learn through inquiry, experience, collaboration, and engagement with real life. John Dewey described the school as a social center. Settlement houses, community-education initiatives, and later experiential and project-based schools carried this model into communities across the country.

Its contribution

Progressive education established the intellectual foundation for student-centered learning, interdisciplinary projects, teacher-as-coach, reflection, and public problem-solving. It changed the image of the learner from a passive recipient into an active participant. Its unfinished work was institutional: authentic learning often depended on an exceptional teacher or school and rarely produced a durable civic relationship or externally recognized credential.

Community control

The national model

Black freedom schools, Indigenous education movements, bilingual education campaigns, parent organizing, and struggles for local school governance treated education as a question of self-determination. From Mississippi Freedom Schools to community-control campaigns in New York, Chicago, Los Angeles, and other cities, families demanded authority over curriculum, staffing, resources, culture, and institutional priorities.

Its contribution

Community control named the political question that instructional reform often avoids: who decides what education is for and whose knowledge counts? It expanded culturally grounded learning, parent leadership, community organizing, and demands for public accountability. But local voice was frequently separated from adequate resources, professional capacity, and durable operating infrastructure. Formal participation did not automatically translate into shared power or better learning.

Small schools and Deeper Learning

The national model

The Coalition of Essential Schools, Annenberg-supported networks, the national small-schools movement, and later organizations such as Big Picture Learning, EL Education, High Tech High, Internationals, Linked Learning, and New Tech Network built coherent alternatives to comprehensive high schools. Hewlett’s Deeper Learning initiative connected ten established networks through a shared set of competencies and a national community of practice.

Its contribution

These movements showed that students can undertake sustained projects, internships, exhibitions, collaborative inquiry, and complex problem-solving while being known well by adults. They created strong school models, professional networks, and a common language for transferable capabilities. Yet even powerful exhibitions usually remained school-based assessments. The movements did not build a common system through which communities validate public contribution and employers or colleges recognize the resulting evidence.

Community Schools

The national model

Community Schools organize public schools as neighborhood hubs. Across urban, suburban, rural, and Tribal communities, schools partner with families, nonprofits, health providers, public agencies, and local institutions to coordinate integrated supports, expanded learning time, family engagement, and collaborative leadership. Federal, state, district, and philanthropic investment has helped move the approach from isolated examples toward a national strategy.

Its contribution

Community Schools created the strongest institutional platform yet for sustained school-community partnership. Coordinators, partner agreements, shared planning, and recurring relationships help communities address the conditions that shape learning. But many implementations concentrate on services delivered to students rather than consequential work undertaken with them. The next frontier is to connect this partnership infrastructure to authentic civic work, public demonstration, external validation, and portable credentials.

Community Schools provide the strongest institutional base yet. The next step is to move beyond coordinating services around students and create a structure through which students contribute to the civic and economic life of their communities.

Two community-school opportunities

One funded state strategy. One citywide delivery network.

California opportunity

The model: The California Community Schools Partnership Program helps school districts build partnerships with families, community organizations, public agencies, and local government. Its framework combines integrated student supports, expanded learning, family and community engagement, and collaborative leadership.

The funding stream: California originally committed more than $4 billion through 2031. Implementation grants can provide eligible school sites as much as $500,000 annually for up to five years. The 2026 state budget added $1 billion in ongoing Proposition 98 funding to sustain and expand the model; approximately 2,500 schools have already received grants.

The Civic Labs opening: California has funded the partnership infrastructure. Civic Labs can give that infrastructure a common learning-and-contribution model—real public work, a community of practice, public demonstration, and externally validated credentials.

New York City opportunity

The model: New York City Community Schools organize schools as neighborhood hubs that support the whole child, the family, and learning inside and outside the classroom. Each school works with community partners and uses extended-day and summer programming to connect services, relationships, and opportunity.

The operating base: The system began with 45 schools in 2014 and now includes 419 Community Schools in every district, supported by city, state, and federal dollars. This is not a pilot network; it is an existing citywide channel capable of reaching students and communities at scale.

The Civic Labs opening: New York already has the schools, coordinators, partners, and public commitment. Civic Labs can add the missing layer for authentic civic work and portable community-learning credentials—beginning with the Bronx and spreading through the 419-school network.

The real gap

The challenge is not more curriculum. It is the capacity to use it together.

In The American Civic Education Ecosystem: A Landscape Analysis, Tom Schnaubelt and his Stanford colleagues found that civic curriculum is already saturated. What educators lack is coherence, implementation support, professional learning communities, and practical opportunities to build the capacity for difficult conversations. The report also finds civic education too siloed across K–12, higher education, and civil society. In a polarized world, the central civic skill is not simply knowing more; it is learning how to engage constructively across ideological, geographic, racial, and socioeconomic differences.

That diagnosis exposes a structural gap. Schools have curriculum, teachers, students, and learning time, but they cannot create this capacity alone. They need trusted spaces where young people, educators, community members, practitioners, and people with different perspectives can work on consequential questions together. Communities hold those relationships, differences, public problems, and lived forms of knowledge—but often lack a reliable way to connect them to learning, evidence, and credentials.

The missing piece is the civic infrastructure that connects them: a repeatable way to bring public questions into schools, organize authentic work with community partners, support a community of practice, present learning in public, validate contribution outside the classroom, and issue a portable credential. Civic Labs are the distribution, coordination, and recognition system that turns civic education into civic participation.

A Civic Lab begins with a real public question and ends with more capacity to address the next one. The issue changes; the infrastructure remains.

The missing civic infrastructure is the system that connects authentic community work, real-world project-based learning, work-based learning, civic education, public demonstration, and external validation. Artificial intelligence makes that connection urgent. Learning can no longer be defined mainly as acquiring content, mastering a body of knowledge, or even synthesizing information; machines can increasingly retrieve and synthesize faster than people can. The distinctively human work is applying knowledge in consequential situations, exercising judgment where values and interests conflict, working with others across differences, and accepting responsibility for what a decision produces. That kind of learning is socially constructed: it requires communities with real questions, schools that provide time and developmental support, practitioners who contribute expertise, and public audiences that can challenge and validate the work. It cannot be reduced to an individual student working privately with a machine. Civic Labs provide the connective structure that turns knowledge into application, application into public contribution, and contribution into evidence that a community—and not only a classroom—can recognize.

Mutual dependence

The assets schools, communities, and young people bring to the table

Young people are not the recipients of this system; they are its essential participants. They bring lived experience, questions adults may no longer see, relationships across school and neighborhood, imagination about what could be different, and the greatest stake in what comes next. Without their agency and contribution, there is no authentic civic learning—only another program designed by adults for young people.

What young people and schools bring

  • Young people’s experience, questions, creativity, and agency
  • Teachers, learning time, and developmental support
  • Academic standards and assessment
  • Safeguarding and institutional continuity
  • A pathway to academic credit

What community partners bring

  • Real questions and consequential work
  • Relationships, history, and lived knowledge
  • Practitioners, employers, and decision-makers
  • External judgment and public accountability
  • A reason for the work to matter

But mutual dependence remains abstract until contribution becomes visible. Today, institutions are good at recording enrollment, attendance, grades, services, and transactions; they are much less capable of seeing who helped whom, what knowledge moved between institutions, what public value was created, or how a young person’s work changed a community. When contribution is invisible, people encounter one another as clients, recipients, competitors, or demographic categories—not as capable partners and co-creators.

Contribution creates ownership. When people can see what they contributed, see that others received and valued it, and connect that contribution to a shared outcome, they develop a legitimate stake in the work and in one another. Public demonstration, external validation, and the Community Learning Ledger make that reciprocal value visible. They allow students, educators, schools, and community partners to recognize themselves as participants in the same civic project.

We do not build belonging by telling people they matter. We build it by making their contribution consequential, visible, and valued.

Reciprocal verification

The double-entry Community Learning Ledger

The Community Learning Ledger is the product: a values-aligned, dual-sided record connecting what a learner contributed with what a community received and valued. One side documents the public contribution; the other documents the learning, application, and judgment demonstrated through the work. By asking schools and community partners to recognize the same evidence together, the ledger makes authentic learning visible and gives the resulting credential meaning beyond the classroom.

The opportunity

Build the field, prove the transaction, and create a market that can sustain it.

The opportunity is not another stand-alone curriculum or short-lived fellowship. Civic Labs + Community Schools are the service: they organize the field, align partners, structure authentic public work, and secure outside validation. The Community Learner Ledger is the product: it records verified contribution and turns it into a portable credential.

The work develops across three phases. Philanthropy can provide catalytic capital at the beginning, while government, business, colleges, universities, and philanthropy become paying institutional customers as the model proves its value. The Civic Tax ensures that every paid use compensates contributors, maintains the shared infrastructure, and leaves a permanent asset behind in community.
