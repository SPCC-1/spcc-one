Turn Community Schools into Civic Infrastructure.

Community Schools already connect young people with educators, families, community organizations, and neighborhood resources. Civic Labs use the platform we already have to turn those relationships into rigorous learning, useful public work, and valuable community assets.

Civic Labs in action

Start with an issue the community is already facing.

A Civic Lab is not a simulation or a fixed curriculum. It begins with a live question named by a community and a real institution prepared to implement the solution—then organizes young people, educators, and partners to investigate the evidence, weigh competing values, and produce work that the identified institution can use.

Food affordability, step by step

00 · Start with the public issue

New York City has committed $70 million to public grocery stores as a response to food affordability. Students begin by asking what this investment should make possible, who it should serve, and how residents should help shape the decisions that follow.

01 · Begin with community

Students study Hunts Point as it exists—not as an abstract food-access problem. They walk the neighborhood, document stores and transit routes, examine prices and availability, and learn from residents, families, bodega owners, and small-business owners about how people actually find and afford food.

02 · Ask better questions

Students turn what they observe into an investigation. They ask how public investment, land, ownership, transportation, wages, pricing, and neighborhood power shape food affordability—and what questions city planners, store operators, and community organizations need to answer.

03 · Listen and gather evidence

Students conduct participatory research with people closest to the issue. They interview bodega owners and small-business owners, meet with city officials and other stakeholders, study data and policy, compare perspectives, and treat lived experience as essential evidence—not merely as anecdote.

04 · Make sense together

Students bring their findings back to the community and work across differences to interpret them. Together, they test assumptions, identify tradeoffs, distinguish what is known from what remains uncertain, and develop conclusions rigorous enough to guide public decisions.

05 · Create a public response

Students present their findings to stakeholders with the authority to act, including city agencies, public officials, community organizations, and potential operators or investors. They create a usable artifact—a recommendation, map, business model, design, report, or decision framework—that others can use to make consequential choices.

06 · Return value to the community

Public and private investments should be shaped by the evidence and contributions of the communities involved. The result should grow community assets and leave behind knowledge, tools, relationships, or institutions that remain useful after the project ends.

The community-school market already exists

A national platform—with mature systems in New York City and California.

Community Schools are not a new market to create. Across the country, federal grants, state appropriations, district budgets, local partnerships, and philanthropy already support schools designed to connect learning with families, public agencies, and neighborhood institutions. The market is national and funded at every level. New York City and California show what becomes possible when that public commitment reaches scale.

The civic ecosystem analysis

The field has civic learning organizations. It lacks the capacity to act together.

In The American Civic Education Ecosystem: A Landscape Analysis, Tom Schnaubelt, Karina Kloos, and Connor Begovich identify three connected capacity gaps:

  • Curriculum is abundant. Coherence is not. Educators face a saturated field of materials without a shared way to select, connect, and use them around consequential public work.
  • The ecosystem remains siloed. K–12 schools, higher education, community organizations, and civic institutions work in parallel rather than as one learning system.
  • Constructive engagement requires practice. Young people need real opportunities to work across ideological, racial, geographic, and socioeconomic differences—not only learn about them.

What Civic Labs add to Community Schools

Growing the shared capacity of our public institutions.

Civic Labs turn a consequential community issue into organized work and a lasting public asset.

01 ·IssueGrounded in the community

A Civic Lab begins with a consequential public issue identified with the people and institutions closest to it. Students and educators study the place as it exists, listening to residents, families, community organizations, workers, and local institutions.

The issue gives the work direction. It establishes why the learning matters, who should be involved, what evidence is needed, and what kind of public response could be useful.

02 ·WorkAligning the ecosystem

The work brings students, educators, community partners, higher-education participants, public agencies, businesses, and other stakeholders together around a shared investigation.

Participants gather and interpret evidence, deliberate across differences, test assumptions, and produce something tangible: a recommendation, map, report, design, business model, policy framework, dataset, story, or other public artifact that an institution or community can use.

03 ·ValueMake the contribution count

Value begins when the work is made public and taken seriously. Students demonstrate what they learned and defend the quality of their findings before stakeholders with the authority to respond.

The contribution is recorded—who created it, what evidence supports it, how it was evaluated, which community and institution received it, and what public value it produced. The result is durable evidence for the learner, a reusable asset for the community, and the possibility of institutional change.

From contribution to infrastructure

From verified contribution to civic infrastructure.

Civic Labs produce verified contribution. The product makes it ownable, usable, and cumulative. Institutional adoption turns community assets into civic infrastructure.

01 · Service

Civic Labs + Community Schools

The connective tissue between grassroots work and institutions.

Civic Labs coordinate the relationships, facilitation, and delivery that carry community knowledge and local work into schools, public agencies, higher education, businesses, philanthropy, and other institutions.

The service brings the right people together, supports the work, and carries the resulting contribution to an institution prepared to recognize and use it.

Produces: a verified contribution.

02 · Product

Community Learning Ledger

A protocol and platform for recording contribution.

The Ledger records what was created, who contributed, what evidence supports it, how the work was evaluated, which community and institution received it, and what permissions govern its use.

The product makes a verified contribution ownable by the learner, usable by institutions and communities, and cumulative across projects, places, and time.

Produces: a portable record and durable community asset.

03 · Market

Institutional adoption

The people and institutions that pay for and legitimize the infrastructure.

Government, higher education, business, philanthropy, schools, and other institutions pay for the infrastructure and knowledge they use.

They provide legitimacy by receiving the work, validating its quality, using it in real decisions, and investing in the assets communities create. Adoption gives those assets standing beyond the original project and creates demand for the next cycle.

Creates: the conditions under which community assets become civic infrastructure.
