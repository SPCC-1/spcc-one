The argument at the center of the American experiment

How do we preserve local voice while building the shared capacity to act together?

Concentrated power can become distant, rigid, and unaccountable. Decisions made far from the people living with their consequences can erase local knowledge, weaken agency, and turn participation into consultation after the fact.

Fragmented power can leave communities isolated and institutions working in parallel. Knowledge remains trapped inside projects and organizations, successful practices struggle to transfer across sectors, and every generation is forced to begin again.

Civic Labs connect local control with collective capacity. Communities define the consequential questions. Schools and young people organize rigorous public work around them. Receiving institutions validate and use what is created. The result remains a community asset while becoming part of a larger civic commons.

Civic Labs in action

Start with a question the community is already facing.

A Civic Lab is not a simulation or a fixed curriculum. It begins with a live question named by a community and a real institution prepared to receive the answer—then organizes young people, educators, and partners to investigate the evidence, weigh competing values, and produce work that institution can use.

Civic Lab 01 · Food affordability

Who Gets to Eat Affordably?

New York City is investing public money in a grocery store in Hunts Point. Students investigate who the investment serves, what tradeoffs it creates, and what an equitable food system should require—then defend a recommendation grounded in community evidence.

Civic Lab 02 · AI in schools

Who Should Govern AI in NYC Schools?

As New York City tests new rules for generative AI, students examine learning, privacy, equity, agency, ownership, and public value. Their charge is to propose a decision-ready framework for what the city should permit, protect, and require.

What makes the model different

The learning practices are established. The civic architecture is different.

Civic Labs repurpose what educators already know: Deeper Learning, 21st-century skills, Habits of Mind, project-based learning, real-world learning, work-based learning, service learning, and civic education. The innovation is not a new pedagogy. It is a different source of demand, a real receiver for the work, and a way to preserve both forms of value created.

The community defines the entry point. The work begins with what residents, community organizations, or public institutions identify as consequential—not simply what a student or teacher chooses as an interesting topic.

A receiving institution stands on the other side. A community organization, agency, school system, college, foundation, or business agrees to hear the work, test its quality, and determine whether it creates public value.

The work produces two assets. The learner gains a credential and portable evidence for an educational and workforce pathway. The community gains useful knowledge that it owns, retains, and can build upon.

The community-school market already exists

A national platform—with mature systems in New York City and California.

Community Schools are not a new market to invent. Across the country, federal grants, state appropriations, district budgets, local partnerships, and philanthropy already support schools designed to connect learning with families, public agencies, and neighborhood institutions. The market is national and funded at every level. New York City and California show what becomes possible when that public commitment reaches scale.

The opportunity is to begin inside two of the country’s most developed Community School systems—one citywide and one statewide—then build for a national field.

New York City · A citywide channel

The scale: New York City’s system began with 45 schools in 2014 and now reaches 419 Community Schools across every district. This is not a pilot network; it is an existing citywide channel supported by city, state, and federal dollars.

The operating model: Each school works with a lead community partner and uses extended-day, summer, family, and student-support infrastructure to connect learning with neighborhood relationships and opportunity.

The Civic Labs opening: New York already has the schools, coordinators, partners, and public commitment. Civic Labs can add the missing layer for authentic civic work, beginning with live Bronx questions and extending through the citywide network.

Explore NYC Community Schools ↗

California · A statewide funding strategy

The scale: Approximately 2,500 California schools have received community-school grants, creating a statewide base of schools, districts, families, public agencies, and community organizations.

The funding stream: California has committed $4.1 billion to the nation’s largest state-level community-school initiative. Eligible sites can receive implementation grants of as much as $500,000 annually for up to five years.

The Civic Labs opening: California has financed the partnership infrastructure. Civic Labs can give it a common learning-and-contribution method: current public questions, rigorous community work, public demonstration, external validation, and durable evidence.

The civic ecosystem analysis

The field has civic learning organizations. It lacks the capacity to act together.

In The American Civic Education Ecosystem: A Landscape Analysis, Tom Schnaubelt, Karina Kloos, and Connor Begovich identify three connected capacity gaps:

  • Curriculum is abundant. Coherence is not. Educators face a saturated field of materials without a shared way to select, connect, and use them around consequential public work.
  • The ecosystem remains siloed. K–12 schools, higher education, community organizations, and civic institutions work in parallel rather than as one learning system.
  • Constructive engagement requires practice. Young people need real opportunities to work across ideological, racial, geographic, and socioeconomic differences—not only learn about them.

The Civic Learning System

Turn disconnected civic activity into regenerative civic infrastructure that compounds.

The work, institutions, and relationships already exist. What is missing is the connective tissue that lets them function as one civic learning system. The Civic Method organizes consequential community questions into rigorous public work; the Community Learning Ledger verifies the result, recognizes the young person, and preserves the contribution as a community-owned asset others can find, use, and build upon. Together, they turn individual projects into a commonwealth of knowledge: durable civic infrastructure in which each contribution strengthens what the community can know, do, and create next.

The product · The Community Learning Ledger

Turn contribution into an asset that people can own, communities can retain, and institutions can trust.

Schools already know how to record courses, grades, attendance, and individual achievement. They do not yet have a shared way to produce and verify a community asset—to establish what was created, who created it, which institution received it, whether it met a meaningful standard, what public value it produced, who owns it, and how others may build upon it. The Community Learning Ledger supplies that missing infrastructure. It records the same verified contribution twice: as portable evidence in the learner’s journey and as a durable asset in the community’s growing body of knowledge.

A ledger of community assets—a commonwealth of knowledge

Every verified contribution adds more than a credential. Research, recommendations, datasets, oral histories, designs, tools, and other public work enter the ledger as community-owned assets with visible provenance, standards, permissions, and institutional verification. Instead of disappearing at the end of a course or presentation, the work remains findable and usable. Over time, the ledger becomes a cumulative knowledge commons: a record of what a community knows, what its people have made, what value others recognized in it, and how the next person or institution can build upon it.

The learner owns the portable record: durable evidence of authorship, capability, judgment, and contribution that can travel across schools, colleges, work, and civic life.

The community retains the public asset: useful knowledge and tools remain accessible to the people whose questions and experience made the work possible.
