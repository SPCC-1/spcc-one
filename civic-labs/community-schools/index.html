Civic Labs in action

Start with a social issue the community is already facing.

A Civic Lab is not a simulation or a fixed curriculum. It begins with a live question named by a community and a real institution prepared to implement the solution—then organizes young people, educators, and partners to investigate the evidence, weigh competing values, and produce work that the identified institution can use.

Civic Lab 01 · Food affordability

Who Gets to Eat Affordably?

New York City is investing public money in a grocery store in Hunts Point. Students investigate who the investment serves, what tradeoffs it creates, and what an equitable food system should require—then defend a recommendation grounded in community evidence.

Food affordability, step by step

00 · Start with the public issue

Mamdani announces $70M for public grocery stores

New York City makes a public investment to address food affordability. Students begin by asking what the investment should make possible—and who it should serve.

01 · Begin with place

Notice the place

Students look closely at the neighborhood, its histories, and the conditions shaping how families find and afford food.

02 · Name the issue

Ask better questions

They identify what is missing from familiar stories and ask how power, ownership, investment, and public choices shape affordability.

03 · Gather evidence

Listen and gather evidence

Students listen to residents and organizations, study local data, and treat lived experience as essential evidence.

04 · Do the work

Make sense together

They compare perspectives, test assumptions, weigh tradeoffs, and decide what the evidence supports.

05 · Make it public

Create a public response

Students translate their learning into a recommendation, design, report, map, story, tool, or proposal.

06 · Create value

Return value to the community

They share a useful contribution and leave knowledge that the next learner, partner, or decision-maker can build upon.

Step 1 of 7 · Public issue

The community-school market already exists

A national platform—with mature systems in New York City and California.

Community Schools are not a new market to create. Across the country, federal grants, state appropriations, district budgets, local partnerships, and philanthropy already support schools designed to connect learning with families, public agencies, and neighborhood institutions. The market is national and funded at every level. New York City and California show what becomes possible when that public commitment reaches scale.

The civic ecosystem analysis

The field has civic learning organizations. It lacks the capacity to act together.

In The American Civic Education Ecosystem: A Landscape Analysis, Tom Schnaubelt, Karina Kloos, and Connor Begovich identify three connected capacity gaps:

  • Curriculum is abundant. Coherence is not. Educators face a saturated field of materials without a shared way to select, connect, and use them around consequential public work.
  • The ecosystem remains siloed. K–12 schools, higher education, community organizations, and civic institutions work in parallel rather than as one learning system.
  • Constructive engagement requires practice. Young people need real opportunities to work across ideological, racial, geographic, and socioeconomic differences—not only learn about them.

What Civic Labs connect

Three things make the work useful and lasting.

Civic Labs move from an issue, to shared work, to value that remains useful and lasting.

01 · Issue

Real community work and authority

Communities identify the issue. Young people and educators investigate it with the people closest to it and help shape what happens next.

02 · Work

Shared public infrastructure

03 · Value

Visible, reusable evidence and assets

The work produces evidence, recommendations, tools, stories, and other assets that can be recognized, used, and built upon.

Civic Labs connect shared infrastructure, real community authority, and visible, reusable evidence and assets.

The product · The Community Learning Ledger

Turn contribution into an asset that people can own, communities can retain, and institutions can trust.

Schools already know how to record courses, grades, attendance, and individual achievement. They do not yet have a shared way to produce and verify a community asset—to establish what was created, who created it, which institution received it, whether it met a meaningful standard, what public value it produced, who owns it, and how others may build upon it. The Community Learning Ledger supplies that missing infrastructure. It records the same verified contribution twice: as tangible evidence in the learner’s journey and as a durable asset in the community’s growing body of knowledge.

The Civic Method+Community Learning Ledger=Civic infrastructure
One connected side

What schools and young people bring

  • Lived experience, curiosity, creativity, and agency
  • Teachers, learning time, standards, and developmental support
  • Research, analysis, design, and public communication
  • Evidence of learning and a pathway to academic recognition
Shared evidence
The other connected side

What community brings

  • Consequential questions and real public stakes
  • History, relationships, lived knowledge, and local expertise
  • A receiving institution committed to hearing and considering the work
  • External judgment of rigor, quality, usefulness, and public value

A ledger of community assets—a commonwealth of knowledge

Every verified contribution adds more than a credential. Research, recommendations, datasets, oral histories, designs, tools, and other public work enter the ledger as community-owned assets with visible provenance, standards, permissions, and institutional verification. Instead of disappearing at the end of a course or presentation, the work remains findable and usable. Over time, the ledger becomes a cumulative knowledge commons: a record of what a community knows, what its people have made, what value others recognized in it, and how the next person or institution can build upon it.

The learner owns the portable record: durable evidence of authorship, capability, judgment, and contribution that can travel across schools, colleges, work, and civic life.

The community retains the public asset: useful knowledge and tools remain accessible to the people whose questions and experience made the work possible.
