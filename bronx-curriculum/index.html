The learning journey

Choose a door.
Keep asking.

This is the same curriculum your teachers use, organized around the questions students are trying to answer. You can move in order or follow the question that has your attention. The goal is not only to understand what happened—it is to decide what your understanding makes possible now.

01
STORY

What story are we given?

Look at films, headlines, photographs, and the words outsiders have used to describe the Bronx.

What you will do

Use I see / I think / I wonder. Compare a dominant narrative with a counter-narrative. Ask what each story leaves out.

Make: a narrative comparison · a question board
Enter the Story unit →
02
HISTORY

How did the Bronx become this place?

Trace Indigenous history, land, slavery, migration, neighborhood life, and the Bronx before major displacement.

What you will do

Read short sources, build a timeline, study maps, and place local history beside national history.

Make: a timeline · a source note · a map
03
SYSTEMS

Who made these conditions?

Investigate Robert Moses, the Cross Bronx Expressway, segregation, redlining, urban renewal, and disinvestment.

What you will do

Compare decisions and consequences. Ask who had power, who benefited, who paid the cost, and whose voice is missing.

Make: a cause-and-effect map · a defended judgment
04
EVIDENCE

What can we verify?

Use interviews, surveys, maps, water testing, statistics, observation, and multiple perspectives.

What you will do

Separate observation from interpretation. Name your claim, show your evidence, find another voice, and revise.

Make: an evidence set · a scientific or historical paragraph
05
ACTION

What can I contribute now?

Turn learning into an exhibition, design, public story, proposal, panel, zine, or community contribution that invites a next step.

What you will do

Connect history to an issue you care about now. Name the change you want to see, the people you want beside you, and one realistic action you can begin.

Make: a public argument · a coalition · a next step

Your investigation toolkit

Good questions
are a kind of power.

These are the moves you will practice again and again. Hover, tap, or focus a card to see the question become a mini lesson.

01

Observe

What do you notice?

Notice before you interpret. Slow down and collect details from a source.

Try it: List three things you can see, hear, or measure. Add one thing you are unsure about.

02

Question

What do you want to know?

Turn surprise, confusion, or curiosity into a question someone could investigate.

Try it: Ask who, what, where, when, or why. Circle the question that could lead to evidence.

03

Contextualize

What came before?

Place a source in time, place, and systems of power. Events do not happen in isolation.

Try it: Add two events before and after your source on a timeline.

04

Compare

Who sees it differently?

Put two accounts, maps, images, or experiences side by side. Look for agreement and difference.

Try it: Write one thing the sources share and one thing only one source shows.

05

Corroborate

What can you verify?

Check a claim against another source. Evidence can confirm, complicate, or challenge a story.

Try it: Find one source that supports your claim and one that makes you pause.

06

Revise

What changed your mind?

New evidence is a reason to improve your thinking, not a failure. Keep a visible trail of changes.

Try it: Cross out, add, or rewrite one part of your first explanation.

07

Interpret

What does it mean?

Explain why the details matter. Ask what story a source is telling—and what it leaves out.

Try it: Complete this sentence: “This source matters because…”

08

Act

What comes next?

Turn understanding into a next move. Decide who needs to hear, decide, join, or help.

Try it: Name one audience and one action your work could invite.

Your work belongs here

Make the next
piece of the story.

The final product does not have to be a five-page essay. Choose a form that helps another person understand what you found.

01 · Listen

Oral history

Interview someone who knows a place, event, or change in the Bronx.

02 · Map

Neighborhood atlas

Connect a story to a street, building, river, store, park, or institution.

03 · Show

Exhibition

Bring Humanities and STEAM evidence together in a public-facing argument.

04 · Imagine

Proposal

Design a public space, policy, program, or next step for the community.

Before you share: Name your sources. Make room for complexity. Ask whose perspective is missing. Get consent for people, photographs, and recordings.
