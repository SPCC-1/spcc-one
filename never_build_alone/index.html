The inheritance

My father died when I was nineteen. He was larger than life, and I never got to know him as a man.

He was an educator, principal, superintendent, public advocate, and writer. I knew his public force. I did not have the time to know the man inside it—or to ask the questions that might have made him more human to me.

But the inheritance is larger than the distance between a father and son. I was born into the America of the 1960s and 1970s: Puerto Rican migration, community power, public education, and the belief that institutions could widen the circle of opportunity.

Over the fifty years since, that promise has frayed. The institutions that once made upward mobility feel possible began to fracture. We lost our way—not because the dream was too large, but because we forgot the relationships, civic responsibility, and shared foundations that made it possible.

Never Build Alone asks what we are missing, and what it would take to recover it.

Why me
“I have spent my life learning how to see the capital already present in communities—and building the institutions that help people turn it into shared power.”

ESO Ventures was the most ambitious expression of that work. It brought together entrepreneurs, capital, public purpose, relationships, and institutional trust. It showed what becomes possible when an ecosystem can organize its own capacity.

It also exposed the question I had not yet resolved: How can capital, community ownership, public trust, and personal ambition be held together in the institutions we create? How can governance represent the ecosystem—not only the founders or the people at its center? How can the value created by many people remain available to the people who made it possible?

That is the work I keep returning to. I know how to help an ecosystem form. I have not yet fully solved how to make the institution accountable to that ecosystem—how to make ownership transferable, governance shared, and prosperity regenerative. The challenge is not simply building a successful company. It is building an institution that can outlast its founders without losing the community that gave it life. That is also the challenge our nation is facing.

The idea in public

“Do you love other people's children?”

America is the richest country in the world, but we are not well. Inclusion is not enough when the people and communities who create value remain outside the ownership, capital, and governance of the institutions around them. We need an economic engine that unlocks last-mile capital, creates shared ownership, and circulates value back to the communities that make prosperity possible. Political democracy cannot remain healthy while economic power is concentrated in so few hands.

Why now

We are asking individuals to navigate a crisis that only shared institutions can solve.

Something is wrong beneath the surface of American life. People feel it in the economy, in public institutions, in education, in healthcare, in the uncertainty around work and artificial intelligence, and in the growing distance between what we are told and what we can see. But we keep translating structural problems into private instructions: work harder, adapt faster, get a better credential, become more resilient.

We ask education to produce individual earners while avoiding the harder questions of ownership, economic access, and civic power. We ask people to be good citizens without asking whether they have the stability, voice, or material stake required to participate. We have separated civic life from economic life, even though one determines who can meaningfully take part in the other.

This is an institutional capital crisis: we have resources, intelligence, and people who care, but the pieces are not connected. The work is often trapped in conversation because there is no shared mandate, budget, or structure strong enough to turn good intentions into common action.

Still, the hope is not abstract. It is in the family that finds common ground, the school that becomes a civic anchor, the local network that keeps showing up, and the patient collaboration that turns a possibility into a proof point. Never Build Alone is an invitation to build where we are—and to rebuild the connective tissue of a common life.

A family story becomes a civic argument

A private wound becomes a public vocation.

Never Build Alone is an intergenerational civic memoir: a story about what one generation inherits, what the next tries to build, what breaks along the way, and what remains unfinished.

It is not a family memoir with politics attached. It traces the civic inheritance passed through a family—and through an America transformed between the 1970s and the present. The family story gives the institutional story its emotional stakes. The institutional story gives the family story its public meaning.

The work ahead

An institution is not complete when it succeeds. It is complete when it can regenerate.

The work ahead is not simply to make institutions succeed. It is to make them more responsible to the communities that created them.

We cannot hoard opportunity or resources and call that prosperity. We inherit language, relationships, institutions, wounds, and possibilities from people who came before us. Our responsibility is to leave the next generation with more than we received—not only more money, but more agency, more trust, more knowledge, more durable relationships, and more ways to participate in the decisions that shape their lives.
