Economic Life Is Political Life

The economy is not somewhere else. It is the world we already share—and the world we are constantly deciding how to organize.

← Back to the Selim + Alfredo correspondence · Book outline

The central idea: Economic life is political life because every economy distributes security, opportunity, risk, ownership, and voice. To teach people about democracy, we also have to help them understand the material systems that make democratic participation possible.

The question is not whether you love your children. Most people would answer yes without hesitation. The harder question is: Do you love other people’s children?

That question takes us beyond private feeling and into public life. It asks whether we are willing to care about the conditions that shape a child’s future even when that child is not our own. It asks whether a healthy society can be built around individual success alone, or whether our lives are tied together by institutions, resources, and responsibilities that we hold in common.

This is where economic life becomes political life.

We often treat the economy as if it were a force of nature—something distant, technical, and too complicated for ordinary people to understand. We talk about markets, debt, wages, interest rates, housing, healthcare, and public budgets as though they belong to specialists. But the economy is not somewhere else. It is in the classroom, the family, the neighborhood, and the choices people make every day.

It is present in whether a family can afford rent. It is present in whether a student has a quiet place to study, whether a parent can take time off when a child is sick, whether a neighborhood has a grocery store, and whether a young person can imagine owning anything beyond their labor. It is present in the roads, libraries, schools, parks, businesses, public agencies, and community relationships that make ordinary life possible.

Every one of these arrangements reflects choices about power. Who owns the land? Who controls the company? Who decides where public money goes? Who benefits when property values rise? Who bears the risk when a business closes, a family becomes ill, or a community loses its jobs? These are economic questions, but they are also political questions because they concern voice, authority, fairness, and the distribution of life chances.

“There is no such thing as wealth without wellness. Generational wealth is generational health. You cannot have one without the other. Wealth and health must be in balance. That’s our work.”— Alfredo Mathew, TEDx West Valley College

My own family history made this visible to me. My grandmother Lydia Rosa Mathew came from San Juan to New York in 1927. Over time, our family moved from poverty toward prosperity. But the meaning of that progress was never only individual. The feeling was: if one person made it, we all made it. Success was understood as something that could enlarge the possibilities of a family and a community.

That belief is at the heart of economic democracy. Economic democracy does not mean that everyone receives the same result or that individual initiative no longer matters. It means that people should have a meaningful stake in the systems that shape their lives. They should have knowledge, voice, security, and—where possible—ownership. They should be able to participate in creating value, not merely receive wages after the most important decisions have already been made.

This is why economic democracy is larger than personal finance. Saving money matters, but individual saving cannot by itself repair a system in which assets, land, capital, and compounding returns are concentrated. People need income, but they also need a chance to build assets. Communities need jobs, but they also need institutions that retain value and reinvest it locally. Public systems need budgets, but they also need democratic stewardship.

Education therefore has to do more than prepare students to find a place in the existing economy. Students already inhabit an economy before they can name it. They know which neighborhoods are safe, which families are under pressure, which businesses are disappearing, and which opportunities seem to belong to someone else. The task of education is to help them understand the systems behind those experiences—and to show them that the present arrangement is not the only arrangement possible.

When students study history, they can see that economic life has always been organized through political decisions. Land has been distributed and taken. Labor has been protected and exploited. Public goods have been created, neglected, privatized, and defended. Communities have organized to demand rights, build institutions, and share resources. The past gives students patterns for understanding the present.

A classroom can then become more than a place where students memorize how the world works. It can become a place where they ask who built the world they inherited, who benefits from it, who is excluded, and what they might help build next.

This does not require teachers to turn every lesson into a political argument. It requires them to acknowledge that education already has political consequences. To teach only individual achievement while ignoring housing, health, ownership, work, and public investment is also a political choice. It tells students that the conditions around them are private problems rather than shared questions.

The alternative is not indoctrination. It is inquiry. Students can study the evidence, listen to the people affected, compare competing explanations, and develop the discernment to recognize both genuine possibility and empty promises. They can learn that public life is not merely something adults conduct on television. It is the work of understanding how a community survives, grows, and distributes responsibility.

Economic life is political life because we are always deciding what we will make possible for one another. A society’s economy is a record of what it values, whom it trusts, what it protects, and what it is willing to leave behind.

The invitation

Begin with the economy students already inhabit. Ask who owns, who decides, who benefits, and who bears the risk. Then help them imagine institutions in which prosperity is shared, assets are built, and every person has a meaningful role in renewing the commonwealth.
