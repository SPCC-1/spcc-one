Eight Core Themes

A tighter dialogue about education, economic democracy, discernment, ownership, and the work of making ideas public.

← Back to the Selim + Alfredo correspondence

This is a condensed version of the October 15 in-person conversation. The exchange has been lightly edited for clarity and flow: filler, interruptions, repetition, and unrelated personal logistics have been removed, while the central language, examples, and arguments remain Selim’s and Alfredo’s.
Theme 1

Education Must Teach Discernment, Not Political Conformity

Selim

Economic democracy was not a concept I had formally encountered before talking with you. Intuitively, it feels like the way things are meant to be, even if it has only been achieved at different moments and to different degrees. As a teacher and entrepreneur, how did you identify this as something that could help people? How should teachers approach systemic problems instead of turning every issue into a pet project or political cause?

Alfredo

Teachers should not impose their political views on students. What young people need is the ability to look at conflicting points of view and ask what is truthful in a world full of knowledge and information, but very little truth. Everyone is holding a partial truth and acting as though they have the whole truth.

Good education helps people hold opposing points of view, navigate ambiguity, and understand that no one knows exactly what the future holds. Everyone needs to understand politics and the economy—not only political scientists or economists. People need to know how business works, how to start a company, the difference between debt and equity, and the difference between earning a wage and owning an asset.

Theme 2

The Economy Changed, but Education Did Not

Selim

When education shifted toward “college for all,” it became acceptable to push the real world off until later. In an earlier economy, high school prepared many people for work. They learned shop, home economics, and business math because they were expected to live with what they learned.

Alfredo

The world changed. After World War II, we had a manufacturing economy, a robust middle class, and a growing expectation that people would attend college. Then we globalized, financialized, and de-industrialized. We shifted industry abroad and invested in technology, finance, and life sciences.

We built a knowledge economy, told everyone to go to college and learn to code, and now the economy is shifting again. Education, workforce, and entrepreneurship need to be in alignment. People will move in and out of education and work throughout their lives, and they will need to own something rather than rely only on a wage.

Theme 3

Generalists, Specialists, and Economic Citizens

Selim

We moved from expecting people to be generalists to expecting them to specialize in a narrow niche. A specialist may thrive in one environment but become vulnerable when that environment changes. Generalists can adapt across different conditions.

Alfredo

I agree with that general arc, but everything is always in tension. The United States has a tension between centralized authority and decentralization, represented in the curriculum by Hamilton and Jefferson.

There is freedom in being self-sufficient and able to live without a distant system. But a small, isolated community is vulnerable and may never develop the medicine, industry, or infrastructure that requires scale. A centralized state can create capacity, but it can also become too powerful. Economic democracy is about navigating between extremes so that individuals do not become weak and fragmented, and the state does not become powerful enough to destroy us.

Alfredo

We are moving from a highly specialized world toward a more generalized one because people can learn. I can study a subject like MS and become fluent enough to ask good questions, but I still need a doctor to treat it. There is incredible value in domain expertise.

If I have a foundation that teaches me how to learn, I can develop specific expertise, verify what I am hearing, and build relationships with people who know more than I do. I do not have to do everything myself. Socially, we can get a lot done.

Theme 4

Good Education Creates Judgment and “Taste”

Alfredo

If you have learned how to learn, you can develop domain expertise, verify what you are hearing, and talk with people who know more than you do. We can get a lot done socially through relationships and dialogue.

Selim

A well-educated person has a sense of taste: you can read something and say, “That doesn’t smell right,” or recognize that something is probably close to the truth. It is a radar that helps you determine whether you are going in the right direction.

It is not about the proper delivery and transmission of factoids that you spit back out. It is about developing the capacity for human judgment and discernment.

Alfredo

MS is a bad example because I am not a biologist. But if someone mentions history, politics, or entrepreneurship—something I know—I can recognize when the story does not sit right. That is taste and discernment.

Selim

If I read about the Strait of Hormuz, I can draw on what I know about empires and empire-building and recognize when the account does not quite sit right. That is developed taste.

Alfredo

But we also have to be careful not to confuse our interests with what will engage every student. Hamilton and Jefferson may be useful historical examples, but the larger questions are more universal: Who should decide how much time a young person spends on a screen? How much authority should belong to parents, teachers, government, or the student?

Theme 5

Learning Must Connect Knowledge to Real Problems

Selim

Teachers can choose stories and make them valuable to the present. Students need enough foundational knowledge for their opinions to be worth hearing. The question is how teachers build the knowledge base that allows students to argue a point intelligently.

Alfredo

Everything has to be scaffolded. A seventh grader can examine food, ask real questions, conduct an inquiry, and present what they learn. That capacity grows over time.

Education should use everyday experiences and universal questions: Who should decide how much time you spend on a screen? Why is food so expensive? Why do food deserts exist? Where does authority sit— with the mayor, the president, parents, teachers, or the student?

Inquiry-based, interdisciplinary projects with a real audience beyond the teacher can make learning relevant and engaging. Students should use AI because the world of work will expect it. But doing something faster does not mean doing it well. Discernment comes through trial, error, social engagement, and practice.

Selim

I want to challenge the idea of real-world issues. Opinions are easy to produce. Students need explicit knowledge and skills so that their opinions are actually worth hearing. The question is how teachers build the foundation that allows a student to argue a point intelligently.

Alfredo

That is why everything has to be scaffolded. A seventh grader can study food and present a real question, but that work builds over time. In the real world, public-private partnerships and startups are already working on open-ended problems in transportation, public health, water, and homelessness.

When I worked with high school students on design challenges, they worked in teams, received mentors, developed ideas, and presented them to a panel. They were not expected to solve California’s drought or homelessness in one day. They were practicing how to recognize an unmet need, develop a solution, identify a user, and receive feedback. That is not so different from the scientific method.

Alfredo

I saw the same thing when I taught IB history. Students from several schools studied water rights in California and the Middle East, represented different countries, wrote position papers, and debated how Jordan, Israel, and Lebanon might share a vital resource. They were disappointed that they did not solve water in the Middle East in a few hours. But the smartest people in the world have not solved those problems either. Life requires doing the best we can with imperfect information and moving the ball forward.

Learning is a journey, not a destination. The value is not only the grade or the completed assignment. The struggle itself can make you a better person.

Selim

It is significantly easier to scale a passive learning experience: talk to thirty students, show a video, and give a quiz. But the teacher as craftsman is being lost. Teachers need pathways to grow as teachers, not only a path into administration.

Alfredo

The craftsman pathway is not built out. I hit my ceiling in teaching, became an entrepreneur, and began educating multiple schools through programs, companies, and design challenges. I learned how to become a community educator. We should not assume everyone has to leave teaching, but when people hit a ceiling, they need a way to pivot, adapt, and continue growing.

Selim

History teachers also have to work within the realities of AP and IB exams. How can they teach this way and still make sure students can write their document-based questions?

Alfredo

DBQs are useful because they are evidence-based. You can teach immigration through the economic impact of immigration, or teach monopolies through the history of the corporation and the current power of Microsoft, Anthropic, and OpenAI. Start with an essential question, such as: How much power should a private company have over an essential marketplace?

Then teach the historical facts needed to understand the question. A unit on Theodore Roosevelt and antitrust could lead to a DBQ about Microsoft, the European Union, and the growing monopolization of AI. History should be taught through the lens of today, so students can recognize patterns and imagine what might be done differently in the future.

Theme 6

Economic Democracy Needs a Tangible Institutional Form

Alfredo

A community land trust offers a tangible example. In a disinvested community such as South Dallas, land can be held in common. Parcels can come from the city, a land bank, or private contributions. That common land creates a balance sheet and a foundation for housing and businesses that people can own.

The goal is to create an economic engine on top of a common resource. The land remains in trust while people build homes, businesses, and other assets on top of it.

Selim

If I build a restaurant in that community, I may be less likely to worry that my rent will shoot up when the lease ends.

Alfredo

Exactly. Small businesses are pressured by leases and do not own the land beneath them. A land trust could keep the rent stable while returning some portion of the value to the trust.

This is not simply a small grocery-store cooperative model. Cooperatives can work at a small scale, but America has a large global economy. Economic democracy and land trusts need enough scale to make financial sense.

Selim

That is a rare middle ground. People who are far left can see shared benefit, while people who believe in survival of the fittest can see that the model is not simply giving people free things.

Selim

At the individual level, there are trusts and savings mechanisms. You are saying we need something pooled. If you could create the smallest version of what you envision as a proof of concept, where would you do it?

Alfredo

People cannot believe in something they cannot see or experience, so we need to create it on a small scale. The challenge is not only the financial mechanism; it is how we govern resources that are collectively owned.

A commonwealth is a membership model. Contribution has to create ownership, and participation has to be voluntary even if the long-term goal is universal. If resources remain small and fragmented, they do not compound. If we aggregate data and assets above a certain threshold, they can compound faster. That is why long-lived institutions have endowments and why a sovereign wealth fund has to pull resources together.

Selim

So the question is not only whether people can save individually. It is whether a community can create a pooled structure that gives people a common foundation and a reason to participate.

Alfredo

Exactly. We have $43 trillion in public debt, more than $1 trillion in annual interest payments, and a $7.6 trillion annual budget. Wealth has grown, but it is concentrated. A sovereign wealth fund for the people could create a universal floor of economic security that compounds over time.

The model does not have to sit only inside the federal government. Social Security is powerful because everyone contributes, everyone receives a benefit, and the trust structure limits the ability to use the money for other purposes. We need a commonwealth that recognizes that private value is built on public infrastructure—schools, roads, law, and the institutions that make an economy possible.

Theme 7

Dialogue Is the Method

Alfredo

I learn and think best through dialogue. I have a large body of writing, research, transcripts, and ideas, but I need to narrow it down: Who is the audience? What do I want to convey? What is the clearest first piece?

I am not looking for a ghostwriter. I need a thought partner who will listen deeply, ask great questions, challenge my assumptions, and contribute original thinking. The dialogue itself helps me crystallize what I believe.

Selim

I am planting seeds and seeing what grows. I wrote a book, and I have learned how difficult it is to turn knowledge into something another person can receive. A teacher with thirty years of experience may retire with an enormous amount of institutional knowledge, and then it disappears.

I would like to help people take the jumble of knowledge in their heads and polish it so that others can use it. Even if ten percent of it reaches one percent of teachers, that is a collective benefit.

Alfredo

You are enjoying this because you are able to be a teacher. You have the confidence to try things, and I am comfortable attempting things that are very large even when I do not fully understand how they will work. I am chipping away at the idea of a sovereign wealth fund for the people.

Selim

I would like to be able to show another person what we have built and say: this is a conversation, this is the work, and this is how it became visible. Could you teach me how to create something like this so I could replicate it with another writer?

Alfredo

I have organized my writing, essays, blog posts, research, and transcripts into a ledger of my work. With AI and simple websites, I can bring that material into one visible place. The value is in seeing the back and forth together: here are the themes, here are the possible chapters, and here is how the conversation develops.

Theme 8

Exploration Must Produce a Public Outcome

Selim

I understand that your life is in flux and that you do not want to promise a year of writing a book before you know what the project is. But I do not want these conversations to become valuable exploration that ends on a hard drive.

Writing a book is harder than sending out snippets of information. It requires you to polish, wait, and crystallize your thinking until the ideas are expressed as well as you can express them at this moment. The act of doing it has value in itself. It proves that you can do it, gives you credibility, and makes the next one easier.

Alfredo

I would love to write a book, but I am scared. The world is full of opinions, newsletters, and noise. I am trying to understand what I have to share that is valuable, how to package it, how to narrow it, and how to break through.

I have done a lot of writing and have my father’s work, but I need to identify the audience and the easiest book to write for a clear audience.

Selim

The outcome does not have to begin as a full-length book. It could be the first three chapters, a case study, a pamphlet, or a public PDF. We can create something tangible and put it into the world. Then we can decide whether it should become a longer book.

Alfredo

The work can become a website, a case study, a pamphlet, or a book. The important thing is to create a public artifact that captures the value of the dialogue and gives us something to build from.

Selim

Publishing is not only writing. When I made my book, I had to work with a designer, decide how it should look, and learn what typesetting and production required. The vague guesses became more confident guesses. You become a writer, designer, publisher, marketer, and advocate for the work.

Your ideas are evergreen. You are not writing only about a crisis of the moment. You are writing about the balance between the one percent and the ninety-nine percent, a problem that has existed since the French Revolution and before. You may not need millions of readers. A thousand people in the educational and civic world who understand what you are doing could be enough to build from.

Selim

I am doing several things and planting seeds to see what grows. I want to help people who have accumulated wisdom—teachers, practitioners, and community leaders—turn what is in their heads into something other people can receive.

Alfredo

Then the first public artifact can be a demonstration of the method itself: a conversation organized into a website that another person can read, follow, and learn from. It does not have to settle every question. It has to make the thinking visible enough that someone else can enter the conversation.

The through-line: Education should help people develop judgment, understand the economy they live in, and participate in building institutions that distribute ownership and agency. The conversation is the method; a useful public work is the test.
